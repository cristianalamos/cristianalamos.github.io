Working Papers

Networks and the Direction of Innovation

with Lukas Althoff

Abstract for Networks and the Direction of Innovation

The internet began as an effort to connect researchers, first in the US and later globally. This paper studies how the internet transformed scientific discovery. We construct a global dataset on when universities and firms first connected to the internet, starting in 1969, and link it to over 10 million publications and patents with detailed information on authors, content, citations, and references. We find that the internet changed what scientists work on, without changing who they work with. After their university connects, scientists turn to increasingly applied questions. At the same time, firms increasingly rely on those new scientific discoveries in their patenting (but not older discoveries). Firms’ own research becomes more theoretical and thus more similar to university research. Collaboration is largely unaffected. We develop a simple model of scientific information networks that highlights the strength of weak ties: by sharing open questions and solutions, lower communication costs can change the direction of science. Text from 300,000 posts in early online scientific forums sheds additional light on the open exchange of questions and solutions across researchers at universities and firms.

Who Gets to Propose Novel Scientific Ideas?

Abstract for Who Gets to Propose Novel Scientific Ideas?

Novel ideas drive scientific progress, yet their value is particularly difficult to judge in advance. The scientific community may therefore rely on authors’ reputation to judge novel ideas, potentially undervaluing those proposed by less established researchers. This paper studies whether novel ideas are underrated by peer review, relative to their eventual impact. I use 123,000 referee reports on all submissions to a top artificial intelligence conference, from which I extract referees’ assessments of novelty. I measure impact by how often each paper is cited and how often its code is copied on GitHub. I find that novel papers receive higher overall referee ratings, holding constant eventual impact. However, this “novelty boost” is 40% smaller for papers written by authors outside top-20 institutions, and entirely absent for papers written by junior authors. I develop a Bayesian model of (editorial) decision-making under uncertainty, isolating referees’ preferences for novelty from risk aversion. Referees report no greater uncertainty about junior authors’ work, suggesting that seniority bias may explain why their novel ideas are undervalued relative to those of senior authors.

SciNet: The Anatomy of Science

with Lukas Althoff

Website Data Coverage: OpenAI Stanford Impact Labs
Details for SciNet: The Anatomy of Science

A task-level database of scientific research: a comprehensive map of what researchers actually do, broken down by domain, field, subfield, and topic. SciNet enables rigorous, task-level analysis of scientific work by mapping the granular activity structure of science across 6 domains, 34 fields, 318 subfields, and 4,481 topics, with 7,262 released tasks.
